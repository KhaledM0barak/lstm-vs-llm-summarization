Researchers at the University of Toronto have developed a battery electrolyte that
allows lithium-ion cells to charge to eighty percent capacity in under six minutes
without the degradation that normally follows fast charging. The team, led by
materials scientist Priya Raghunathan, published the findings on Tuesday in the
journal Nature Energy. Conventional fast charging forces lithium ions into the
graphite anode faster than they can settle, which causes metallic lithium to plate
on the surface and permanently reduce capacity. The Toronto group added a
fluorinated additive that reorganises how ions cluster near the electrode, allowing
them to enter the anode more evenly. Cells built with the electrolyte retained
ninety-two percent of their original capacity after a thousand fast-charge cycles,
compared with sixty-one percent for the control group. Raghunathan cautioned that
the work has so far been demonstrated only in coin cells a few centimetres across,
and that scaling to automotive formats introduces thermal problems the study did
not address. Industry analysts said the result was promising but early. "Every
year brings a fast-charging breakthrough in a coin cell," said Marcus Vale, an
analyst at Fenwick Energy Research. "The question is always whether it survives
contact with a manufacturing line." The university has filed a provisional patent
and is in discussions with two North American cell manufacturers. Funding came from
the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada. The research group
expects to publish results from larger pouch cells within eighteen months.
